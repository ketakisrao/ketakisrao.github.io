A recent post on "unlearnings" in the AI era really stuck with me. Moving toward staff engineering is rarely about adopting new tools; it is about letting go of the instincts that made you feel competent earlier in your career.

Engineering progression long rewarded heads-down execution: syntax mastery, closed tickets, and grinding through problems alone. As AI absorbs much of that mechanical heavy lifting, it accelerates the shift toward what staff-level engineering has always demanded: judgment, architectural clarity, and durable business outcomes.

Here are the five unlearnings from that post and my engineering lens on them.

1. Unlearn doing everything yourself

This one stings when your professional identity was forged by being the person who can always figure it out alone. At junior and senior levels, writing every line yourself feels like craftsmanship. At staff level, it is a bottleneck.

Here's how I do it:

  • End-to-end vision before delegation: I solidify the initial architecture and decide how each piece fits in before delegating the code generation and scaffolding to AI tools.
  • Focus energy on critical review: I spend my cognitive budget stress-testing edge cases, security boundaries, and failure modes rather than syntax generation.

2. Unlearn equating effort with value

There is a quiet guilt in engineering when something takes thirty minutes instead of three days. We have been conditioned to associate sweat equity with value. But users do not care how many hours you sat in your IDE; they care whether the problem is solved.

Here's how I do it:

  • Instrument telemetry on day one: I measure real post-launch metrics like user adoption, latency, and drop-offs rather than tracking hours logged or tickets closed.
  • Partner closely with data analysts: Numbers tell you what is happening in production. I work with analysts to translate technical performance into business impact narratives that code alone cannot tell.

3. Unlearn waiting until you know how

The urge to feel fully prepared before touching an unfamiliar codebase is deeply wired. Historically, entering an unfamiliar domain meant wading through mounds of stale, disjointed docs, hitting permission walls, or scheduling meetings with senior teammates just to get oriented. Today, AI tools make it frictionless to cut through that noise and extract personalized, context-rich information from massive corpuses of internal documentation, surfacing exactly what matters to you and your specific use case.

Here's how I do it:

  • Extract personalized insights from mounds of docs: Instead of manually sifting through hundreds of wiki pages and RFCs, I prompt AI tools to synthesize internal documentation directly against my exact use case, constraints, and service dependencies.
  • Converse with the codebase directly: Before filing a ticket or scheduling a sync, I query internal knowledge bases and repo contexts to test assumptions and build end-to-end mental models in minutes.

4. Unlearn protecting your expertise by staying in your lane

Honestly, the "stay in your lane" mindset bothered me even as a junior engineer. Breadth matters. Understanding the full spectrum makes you sharper, not less specialized.

Here's how I do it:

  • Trace the full request lifecycle: When working on frontend code, I do not stop at the UI; I dig into the API gateway, database queries, and caching layers to see the whole system.
  • Design for platform reusability: I look for patterns that can be abstracted into shared primitives, making it easy for multiple squads to solve the same problem reliably.

5. Unlearn decomposing work by role or ticket

Senior engineers complete Jira tickets efficiently. Staff engineers look beyond the ticket to the company's broader trajectory.

Here's how I do it:

  • Tie engineering choices to business health: Before writing a spec, I ask whether this will improve NPS, customer trust, revenue, or retention.
  • Sit in on user research: I watch real users interact with our features to see where they hesitate, ensuring technical designs fit human mental models rather than system convenience.
  • Co-design telemetry with data analysts: I involve data analysts during early architecture and data modeling to decide exactly how key metrics will be tracked. Thinking across engineering, product, and data perspectives up front ensures we measure true impact rather than scrambling for retroactive analytics.

Stepping into Staff Thinking

The step from senior to staff engineering is notoriously tricky because it requires setting aside the very instincts that brought you early success:

Less execution, more judgment.
Less ownership of individual tasks, more ownership of outcomes.
Less "I built this," more "this is why it mattered."

Working alongside AI doesn't diminish engineering; it simply reallocates where our attention goes. When syntax, scaffolding, and initial drafts take minutes instead of days, the work that remains (architectural judgment, deep empathy for users, cross-functional alignment, and strategic clarity) is what engineering leadership has always been about.

Unlearning old habits is uncomfortable, but it makes space for the kind of work that truly moves teams and products forward.
